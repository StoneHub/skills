# The creative grill

A promo lives or dies on the idea, not the renderer. Fifteen minutes of sharp back-and-forth before any code usually beats three rounds of revisions after a render. The goal of the grill is to find the one true, specific, slightly surprising thing to say, and the single best way to show it.

Run it as a conversation, not a form. Ask a few questions at a time, offer options to react to (people are better editors than authors), push back on vague answers, and bring your own opinions. If the user says "just make it," do a silent self-grill (below) and go. You can always grill on the second version.

## Round 1: the truth (what's real)

Get answers to these, from the user or from your own research. Prefer evidence you can find (repo, site, docs, Slack, past posts) over asking.

- **Who exactly is watching, and where?** For example: a plaintiff-firm intake manager scrolling LinkedIn, or an Android dev on X. Platform drives aspect ratio, length, and whether sound is on.
- **What's the pain, in their words?** The best hooks quote a frustration the viewer has actually felt. Ask for the founder's or creator's own story. It's usually the strongest material, and it's theirs to tell.
- **What should they believe or do after watching?** One action: trial, download, star, book a demo.
- **What's the proof?** Real UI, real numbers, real customers, a real demo. Which claims are approved, and where are they published?
- **What must it not say?** Legal, compliance, competitor sensitivities, unreleased features, customer names.
- **Which assets exist?** Logo, icon, fonts, colors, screenshots, recordings, testimonials, past videos. Go find them before asking.
- **Sound:** silent-first captions, music, SFX, voiceover (whose voice, what tone)?

## Round 2: three different concepts

Pitch three concepts that differ in kind, not just in color. For each: a working title, the hook (first 2–3 seconds, literally what's on screen), the turn (where the product enters), the ending, and the risk. Useful archetypes to pull from (and to break):

- **Problem → agitation → relief.** Show the pain at its most relatable, twist the knife a bit, then resolve with the product.
- **"They never say X."** An insight only this company knows, from its data or experience.
- **Founder POV.** "I built this because…", first person, honest, a little funny.
- **Parody / exaggeration.** A clearly comic dramatization of the old way. The joke has to land with sound off.
- **Speed run.** Show the whole workflow faster than the viewer expects. Only if the speed is real.
- **Numbers as heroes.** Big, verified stats with kinetic counters. Works best as a middle act, not the hook.
- **Demo as story.** One real task end to end; the product does the talking.
- **Before/after split screen.**
- **Wildcards:** 3D, data art, generative visuals, a song, a character, a fake movie trailer. Propose at least one bold option the user wouldn't have thought of.

Recommend one, and say why.

## Round 3: grill the chosen concept

Pressure-test it. Ask yourself and the user:

- Would a stranger get it in 3 seconds with the sound off?
- What's the single frame someone would screenshot or pause on? If there isn't one, design it.
- What will a skeptic in the comments say? ("Works fine for me." "That's not how intake works." "Fake demo.") Pre-empt it or change it.
- Is every claim verifiable and approved? Is anything dramatized, and is that clear?
- Is it the company's voice, or generic ad-speak? Cut any line that could appear in a competitor's ad.
- Is there one idea per scene? What can be cut? (Something can always be cut.)
- Does the ending tell the viewer exactly what to do and where?

## Round 4: beat sheet

Write the beat sheet before building: time range, picture, on-screen text, sound or VO. Keep it short enough that the user can approve it in one read. Lock the VO script here if there is one; the VO will set the timing.

## Silent self-grill (when the user wants speed)

Do the rounds internally: gather the truth from available sources, sketch three concepts in a sentence each, pick one, run the Round 3 checks, and write the beat sheet. Then build, and present the cut along with the two concepts you didn't pick, so the user can redirect cheaply.

## After the first cut

Show a contact sheet or video and ask targeted questions ("Is the hook too long? Does the stamp joke read as mean?") rather than "thoughts?". Offer variants that are cheap to make from the same code: a 15-second cut, vertical 9:16, alternate hooks, alternate CTA.
